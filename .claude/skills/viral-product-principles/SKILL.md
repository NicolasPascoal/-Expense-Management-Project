# 32 Principles of a Viral Product

## Purpose

A checklist for evaluating or drafting the parts of a product that face new visitors:
landing page, pricing, headline, hero section, OG image, CTA, comparison table. Apply as
a review pass — flag violations, don't rewrite blindly; some tradeoffs are deliberate
(e.g. a genuine freemium strategy is a business decision, not automatically a mistake).

## The 32 Principles

**Pricing & business model**
1. No free plan — free users rarely convert (<3%) and inflate support/infra cost. Question free tiers; ask if they're intentional.
2. Hard paywall — ask for payment before asking for data/signup, if validation is the goal.
3. Popcorn Pricing — three tiers max: Good, Better, Best. More tiers = more hesitation.
4. No subscription unless the product genuinely requires recurring value delivery — one-time payments sell easier.
5. Price higher than competitors, not lower — nobody talks about the second-cheapest option.
6. Pricing is easy to find — put "Pricing" in the header nav.
7. Let people try core functionality before paying — don't wall off the features that prove value.

**Visual design**
8. Three colors max — black text, white background, one accent color for the buy button.
9. One idea per screen — each screen/section communicates exactly one thing.
10. OG image designed like a YouTube thumbnail — it's seen more than the site itself.

**Copy & messaging**
11. Numbers over adjectives — "saves 4 hours/week" beats "fast."
12. Headline a fifth grader understands — no jargon, no clever wordplay that obscures meaning.
13. Copy only this founder could write — from lived experience, not swappable with a competitor's.
14. Steal phrasing from actual customer quotes/reviews — they describe the value better than the team does.
15. No weak/hedging words — "most," "many," "rarely" are unfalsifiable; make concrete claims.
16. Headline should be memorable a day later — test candidates, keep the one that sticks.
17. Emotional headline — aim for a laugh, a "wow," or genuine surprise, not a feature description.
18. Product name uses plain, familiar words — avoid invented words or puns needing explanation.
19. Sell the underlying human desire (time, money, status, health, less pain) — features are just the vehicle.
20. Description fits in under 10 words — if the team can't say it that short, visitors won't repeat it.

**Structure & flow**
21. Strong footer — most visitors won't buy but might share; the last thing they see should be shareable/memorable.
22. Show the product before explaining it — a demo/screenshot beats paragraphs of description.
23. Product does one thing — broad tools are forgettable; a single sharp use case is not.
24. Hero section alone should be enough to sell — ~80% of visitors never scroll further.
25. Empathy before the pitch — describe the visitor's problem better than they could, before proposing the fix.
26. One call to action — every extra button/link splits attention and increases the chance of none being clicked.
27. CTA states the next concrete action ("Analyze My Website"), never a vague verb ("Get Started").
28. Testimonials/proof exist before driving traffic — don't send visitors to a page with zero social proof.
29. Comparison table vs. named competitors — make the reason to switch obvious, not implied.

**Positioning & timing**
30. Ride an existing wave — attach to a trend, technology, or conversation people already have.
31. Do something visibly new — a visual/mechanical clone of an existing product gives nobody a reason to share it.
32. Founder is visible (face/voice) — a screen recording or short founder video outperforms a produced corporate promo.

## How to apply

- When reviewing an existing page: go principle by principle, cite the specific section that violates it, propose a concrete rewrite (not just "make it punchier").
- When drafting from scratch: start from #24 (hero must sell alone) and #12 (fifth-grade headline) — get those two right before anything else.
- These are conversion heuristics, not universal law. A B2B enterprise tool legitimately needs different treatment than a consumer landing page — say so when a principle doesn't fit the context instead of forcing it.
